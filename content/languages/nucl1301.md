# Turkish

## Hook

On 1 November 1928, a law of eleven articles switched Turkish from Arabic letters to a Latin-based alphabet [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
Newspapers, shop signs and advertisements had to use the new letters from December 1928, about four weeks after the law was published [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

## Story

### 8th century: the Orkhon inscriptions (Old Turkic context)

The oldest long Turkic texts stand in central Mongolia, near the old course of the Orkhon River [@kacalin2007_orhon].
They honour Kül Tigin (about 732), Bilge Kağan (about 735) and the statesman Tonyukuk (716 to 734) [@kacalin2007_orhon].
They are carved in the Old Turkic script, which Vilhelm Thomsen deciphered and published in 1896 [@kacalin2007_orhon].

The Kül Tigin stone opens its east face with a picture of the world [@kacalin2007_orhon]:

This is context, not a grandparent.
Glottolog places Turkish in the Oghuz branch of Common Turkic [@glottolog53_nucl1301].
It keeps "Old Turkish" (ISO otk) only as a retired bookkeeping entry, outside its tree [@glottolog53_oldt1247].

### 1072 to 1074: Mahmud al-Kashgari's dictionary

Mahmud al-Kashgari began the Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk (ديوان لغات الترك) in 1072 and finished it in 1074 [@kacalin1994_divan].
He presented it in Baghdad, probably in 1077, to Abu'l-Qasim Abdullah, son of the caliph al-Muqtadi [@kacalin1994_divan].
The headwords are Turkic and the explanations are in Arabic [@kacalin1994_divan].
The dates inside the book do not agree, and scholars still argue about them [@kacalin1994_divan].

For Turkish, the key detail is the Oghuz material.
Kashgari records 185 words he assigns to the Oghuz dialect [@kacalin1994_divan].
Only Khaqani Turkic gets more words in the dictionary [@kacalin1994_divan].
The book also contains a map of the Turkic world centred on Balasagun [@kacalin1994_divan].

| Arabic script | Transliteration | Translation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| اُلَشْدى | üleşdi | "shared" (modern Turkish paylaştı) |

Sample from the dictionary as cited by Kaçalin [@kacalin1994_divan].
The only known manuscript, copied in 1266, is in the Millet Library in Istanbul [@kacalin1994_divan].

### 13th to early 20th century: Ottoman Turkish

Ottoman Turkish was the written language of the Turks of the Ottoman lands, written in Arabic letters [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
Scholars date it from the 13th century to the early 20th [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
Mustafa Özkan divides it into three periods [@ozkan2007_osmanli]:

- 13th to 15th century: a plain written language, also called Old Anatolian Turkish.
- Second half of the 15th century to the mid-19th: the classical period, centred on Istanbul.
- Mid-19th century to the early 20th: renewal, with newspapers and calls for simpler prose.

Ottoman Turkish borrowed many Arabic and Persian words [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
It also borrowed whole patterns: Persian compound nouns and adjectives, and Arabic derivations, numbers and plurals [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
In the classical period, writers used two levels: a plain style for information, and an ornate style dense with Arabic and Persian [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
Özkan dates the end of classical Ottoman to the 1911 "Yeni Lisan" (New Language) movement [@ozkan2007_osmanli].

### 1928: the alphabet law

Law No. 1353 was adopted on 1 November 1928 and published in the Official Gazette on 3 November [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
Article 1 replaces the Arabic letters used for Turkish with letters "Latin esasından alınan" (taken from a Latin basis) [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
The law calls them "Türk harfleri", Turkish letters [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

The deadlines were short [@tr_kanun1353_1928]:

| From | What had to use the new letters |
| --- | --- |
| December 1928 | Signs, advertisements, cinema text, newspapers and magazines |
| 1 January 1929 | State offices, banks and companies; printed books |
| June 1929 | Title deeds, civil registers, military papers |
| June 1930 | End of the grace period for old printed forms and shorthand in Arabic letters |

Article 9 required schools to teach in the new letters and banned teaching from books in the old ones [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
Old banknotes, shares and legal documents stayed valid until replaced [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

### 1932 onward: the language reform

The Turkish Language Society was founded on 12 July 1932, on Atatürk's instruction, as Türk Dili Tetkik Cemiyeti [@tdk_tarihce].
Its founders were four deputies and writers: Sâmih Rif'at, Ruşen Eşref, Celâl Sâhir and Yakup Kadri [@tdk_tarihce].
Its aim was to bring out the "öz güzelliğini ve zenginliğini" (inherent beauty and richness) of Turkish [@tdk_tarihce].

A campaign to remove words of foreign origin ran until autumn 1935 [@tdk_tarihce].
After that, foreign-origin words already settled in everyday speech were kept [@tdk_tarihce].
The "öz Türkçe" (pure Turkish) question stayed a debate among Turkish intellectuals after Atatürk's death [@tdk_tarihce].
Under Article 134 of the 1982 Constitution, the society was placed under a new state body [@tdk_tarihce].
That body is the Atatürk Kültür, Dil ve Tarih Yüksek Kurumu (Atatürk High Institution of Culture, Language and History) [@tdk_tarihce].

The results show in today's dictionary.
TDK marks mektep (from Arabic) as dated and sends the reader to okul, "school" [@tdk_gts, s.v. mektep; s.v. okul].
It does the same for tayyare (from Arabic), sending the reader to uçak, "aeroplane" [@tdk_gts, s.v. tayyare].

## Sounds

Turkish has eight vowels: four front and four back [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Long [iː], [eː], [uː] and [aː] also occur [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Zimmer and Orgun illustrate them with one minimal set of short words, spoken by a 25-year-old from Istanbul [@zimmer_orgun1992]:

| Letter | IPA | Word | Gloss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| i | [i] | kil [kil] | clay |
| ü | [y] | kül [kyl] | ashes |
| e | [e] | kel [kel] | bald |
| ö | [œ] | göl [gœl] | lake |
| a | [a] | kal [kaɫ] | stay |
| ı | [ɯ] | kıl [kɯɫ] | hair |
| u | [u] | kul [kuɫ] | slave |
| o | [o] | kol [koɫ] | arm |

Notice the l. After front vowels it is a palatalized [l]; after back vowels it is a velarized [ɫ] [@zimmer_orgun1992].

### Vowel harmony

Most suffixes copy features of the vowel before them [@utaustin_handout9].
The plural has two forms, -ler after front vowels and -lar after back vowels [@utaustin_handout9]:

| Singular | Plural | Gloss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ip | ipler | rope |
| el | eller | hand |
| köy | köyler | village |
| yüz | yüzler | face |
| kız | kızlar | girl |
| pul | pullar | stamp |

High-vowel suffixes also copy rounding, so they have four forms [@utaustin_handout9].
The possessive suffix shows this: -sı, -si, -su, -sü [@utaustin_handout9].

| Word | Its ... | Gloss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| oda | odası | room |
| dere | deresi | river |
| boru | borusu | pipe |
| ütü | ütüsü | iron (for clothes) |

The example words above are written in TDK spelling; the source gives them in IPA.

### Signature sounds

- **ı [ɯ]:** a high back unrounded vowel, as in kıl [kɯɫ] "hair" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **ğ, "soft g":** at the end of a word or before a consonant, it lengthens the vowel before it: dağ [daː] "mountain" [@zimmer_orgun1992]. Between vowels it is usually silent; between front vowels it can be a weak glide [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **c and ç:** cam [dʒam] "glass" and çam [tʃam] "pine" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **ş:** güneş [ɟyˈneʃ] "sun" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **k and g with front vowels:** in native words, [c] and [ɟ] appear with front vowels and [k] and [ɡ] with back vowels [@zimmer_orgun1992]. Some loanwords break the pattern: kâr [caɾ] "profit" against kar [kaɾ] "snow" [@zimmer_orgun1992].

### Stress

Stress usually falls on the last syllable [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Some suffixes cannot take stress and push it one syllable left [@zimmer_orgun1992]:
yaptırmalı [jap-tɯr-maˈɫɯ] "s/he must have (it) done", but yaptırmamalı [jap-ˈtɯr-ma-maɫɯ] "s/he must refrain from having (it) done" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Some words are exceptions, such as masa [ˈmasa] "table" [@zimmer_orgun1992].

## Writing

Turkish, and the Turkic texts before it, have used several scripts.

- **Old Turkic script:** used on the Orkhon stones for an earlier Turkic language, not for Turkish itself [@kacalin2007_orhon].
- **Arabic script for Kashgari's Turkic headwords:** in the 11th-century Dīwān [@kacalin1994_divan].
- **Arabic script:** used for Ottoman Turkish until 1928 [@ozkan2007_osmanli; @tr_kanun1353_1928].
- **Latin-based Turkish alphabet:** since the law of 1928 [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
- **Greek script:** TODO(verify) for Karamanli Turkish, which Glottolog lists as a Turkish variety [@glottolog53_nucl1301].

The 1928 table has 29 letters [@tr_kanun1353_1928]:

A B C Ç D E F G Ğ H I İ J K L M N O Ö P R S Ş T U Ü V Y Z

It has no Q, W or X [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

### Four letters to know

- **I ı and İ i:** the table lists dotless I ı and dotted İ i as two separate letter pairs [@tr_kanun1353_1928]. Dotless ı is [ɯ]; dotted i is [i] [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **Ş ş:** [ʃ], as in güneş [ɟyˈneʃ] "sun" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **Ğ ğ:** no sound of its own in standard speech; it lengthens or links vowels [@zimmer_orgun1992].

The dotless and dotted pairs matter for software: uppercase i is İ in Turkish. TODO(verify): cite CLDR or Unicode casing rules; script-typographer to confirm.

## Grammar DNA

1. **Vowel harmony.** Suffix vowels match the stem: kızlar "girls", köyler "villages" [@utaustin_handout9].

2. **Suffixes only, stacked in order.** WALS classes Turkish as strongly suffixing [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 26A].
   yap-tır-malı (do-CAUS-NEC) "s/he must have (it) done" [@zimmer_orgun1992]. Gloss labels: TODO(verify).

3. **The evidential -miş.** Turkish marks whether the speaker saw the event [@dehaan2013_wals77].
   Ahmet gel-di (Ahmet come-PST.DIR.EVD) "Ahmet came" (witnessed) [@dehaan2013_wals77].
   Ahmet gel-miş (Ahmet come-PST.INDIR.EVD) "Ahmet came" (not witnessed) [@dehaan2013_wals77].

4. **Verb last.** WALS records subject, object, verb order [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 81A].
   From the North Wind and the Sun: Derken, kalın bir palto giymiş bir yolcu gördüler [@zimmer_orgun1992].
   "Then they saw a traveller wearing a thick coat." Translation: TODO(verify).

5. **No grammatical gender.** WALS records no gender system [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 30A].
   Example: TODO(verify), a sourced pronoun paradigm.

6. **No definite article; bir for "a".** WALS records an indefinite article but no definite one [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 37A].
   kalın bir palto "a thick coat" [@zimmer_orgun1992].

7. **Postpositions.** Words like "with" follow their noun [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 85A].
   Poyrazla güneş "the north wind and the sun" [@zimmer_orgun1992]. That -la is the reduced form of the postposition ile: TODO(verify).

8. **Question particle.** Yes-or-no questions use a particle [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 116A].
   Example: TODO(verify), a sourced sentence with mi.

9. **Stress that suffixes can move.** Negative -ma cannot take stress, so stress shifts left [@zimmer_orgun1992].
   yaptırmalı [jap-tɯr-maˈɫɯ] against yaptırmamalı [jap-ˈtɯr-ma-maɫɯ] [@zimmer_orgun1992].

10. **Consonants that change before vowels.** In many words a final p, t, ç or k turns voiced before a vowel suffix [@utaustin_handout9].
    kap "container", kaba "to the container"; renk "colour", renge "to the colour" [@utaustin_handout9].
    Other words keep it: sap "stalk", sapa "to the stalk" [@utaustin_handout9].

11. **Long derived words.** Causatives and other suffixes can pile up inside one word [@zimmer_orgun1992].
    paltosunu çıkarttırabilenin, roughly "of whoever could make him take off his coat" [@zimmer_orgun1992]. Segmentation and gloss: TODO(verify).

## Connections

### Family

Glottolog puts Turkish in the Turkic family, Oghuz branch, West Oghuz group [@glottolog53_nucl1301].
Other Turkic languages in this atlas include South Azerbaijani, Northern Uzbek and Kazakh. The Family tab shows where each sits.

### Contact

- **Persian:** everyday words such as pencere, hafta, şehir and dost [@tdk_gts]. Ottoman writers also used Persian compounds [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
- **Arabic:** kitap, kalem, mektep and tayyare [@tdk_gts]. Ottoman Turkish used Arabic derivations and plurals [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
- **Greek:** TDK traces fener and liman to Rumca, the Greek of Anatolia and Istanbul [@tdk_gts, s.v. fener; s.v. liman]. TODO(verify): the gloss of "Rumca".
- **French:** otobüs, from autobus [@tdk_gts, s.v. otobüs].

### A contested deeper family

Some scholars group Turkic with Mongolic, Tungusic, Koreanic and Japonic as "Transeurasian" [@robbeets2021].
Robbeets and colleagues argued in 2021 that the first farmers of Northeast Asia spread these languages [@robbeets2021].
Tian and colleagues replied in 2022 that the evidence supports neither the family nor that spread [@tian2022].
Glottolog treats Turkic as an independent family [@glottolog53_nucl1301]. WALS files Turkish under "Altaic" [@wals2013_tur].
This atlas shows the question as contested.

## Oddity: whistled Turkish

In the steep hills of Türkiye's eastern Black Sea region, some farming families talk across valleys by whistling [@unesco_ich_whistled; @unesco_ich_whistled_nomination].
UNESCO inscribed "Whistled language" of Türkiye on its Urgent Safeguarding List in 2017 [@unesco_ich_whistled].
The nomination names Kuşköy and Karabörk, villages in the Çanakçı district of Giresun, as places where it is widely practised [@unesco_ich_whistled_nomination].
Giresun hosts a Bird Language Festival [@unesco_ich_whistled_nomination]. TODO(verify): the Turkish name of the festival and of the practice (kuş dili).

The whistles carry the words of spoken Turkish, with the same vocabulary and grammar [@bionity2015_whistled].
UNESCO names mobile phones and fading interest among young people as the main threats [@unesco_ich_whistled].

In 2015 a Bochum team tested 31 people from Kuşköy [@bionity2015_whistled].
For spoken syllables they found the usual left-hemisphere advantage; for whistled syllables it disappeared [@bionity2015_whistled].
