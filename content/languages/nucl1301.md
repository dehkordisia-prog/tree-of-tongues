# Turkish

## Hook

On 1 November 1928, a law of eleven articles switched Turkish from Arabic letters to a Latin-based alphabet [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
Newspapers, shop signs and advertisements had to use the new letters from December 1928, about four weeks after the law was published [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

## Story

### 8th century: the Orkhon inscriptions (Old Turkic context)

Some of the oldest long Turkic texts stand in central Mongolia [@kacalin2007_orhon].
The Kül Tigin and Bilge Kağan stones stand near the old course of the Orkhon River [@kacalin2007_orhon].
Tonyukuk's stands about 360 km to the east [@kacalin2007_orhon].
They honour Kül Tigin (about 732), Bilge Kağan (about 735) and the statesman Tonyukuk (716 to 734) [@kacalin2007_orhon].

They are carved in the Old Turkic script.
Vilhelm Thomsen deciphered it in 1893 [@wp-orkhon]. TODO(verify): Thomsen's own 1893 notice; the 1893 date rests on a tertiary source.
His full edition, [Inscriptions de l'Orkhon déchiffrées]{lang=fr}, appeared in Helsingfors in 1896 [@kacalin2007_orhon].

### 1072 to 1074: Mahmud al-Kashgari's dictionary

Mahmud al-Kashgari began the [Dīwān Lughāt al-Turk]{lang=ar-Latn} ([ديوان لغات الترك]{lang=ar}) in 1072 and finished it in 1074 [@kacalin1994_divan].
He presented it in Baghdad, probably in 1077, to Abu'l-Qasim Abdullah, son of the caliph al-Muqtadi [@kacalin1994_divan].
The headwords are Turkic and the explanations are in Arabic [@kacalin1994_divan].
The dates inside the book do not agree, and scholars still argue about them [@kacalin1994_divan].

For Turkish, the key detail is the Oghuz material.
Kashgari records 185 words he assigns to the Oghuz dialect [@kacalin1994_divan].
Only Khaqani Turkic gets more words in the dictionary [@kacalin1994_divan].
The book also contains a map of the Turkic world centred on Balasagun [@kacalin1994_divan].

### 13th to early 20th century: Ottoman Turkish

Ottoman Turkish was the written language of the Turks of the Ottoman lands, written in Arabic letters [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
Mustafa Özkan dates Ottoman Turkish from the 13th century to the early 20th [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
Other scholars treat the 13th to 15th centuries as a separate stage, Old Anatolian Turkish, and start Ottoman later. TODO(verify): Kerslake 1998, not opened.

Özkan divides it into three periods [@ozkan2007_osmanli]:

- 13th to 15th century: a plain written language, which he notes is also called [Eski Anadolu Türkçesi]{lang=tr} (Old Anatolian Turkish).
- Second half of the 15th century to the mid-19th: the classical period, centred on Istanbul.
- Mid-19th century to the early 20th: renewal, with newspapers and calls for simpler prose.

Ottoman Turkish borrowed many Arabic and Persian words [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
It also borrowed whole patterns: Persian compound nouns and adjectives, and Arabic derivations, numbers and plurals [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
In the classical period, writers used two levels: a plain style for information, and an ornate style dense with Arabic and Persian [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
Özkan writes that the 1911 "[Yeni Lisan]{lang=tr}" (New Language) movement ended classical, ornate Ottoman prose [@ozkan2007_osmanli].

### 1928: the alphabet law

Law No. 1353 was adopted on 1 November 1928 and published in the Official Gazette on 3 November [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
Article 1 replaces the Arabic letters used for Turkish with letters "[Latin esasından alınan]{lang=tr}" (taken from a Latin basis) [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
The law calls them "[Türk harfleri]{lang=tr}", Turkish letters [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

The deadlines were short [@tr_kanun1353_1928]:

| From | What had to use the new letters |
| --- | --- |
| December 1928 | Signs, advertisements, cinema text, newspapers and magazines |
| 1 January 1929 | State offices, banks and companies; printed books |
| June 1929 | Title deeds, civil registers, military papers |
| June 1930 | End of the grace period for old printed forms and shorthand in Arabic letters |

Article 9 required schools to teach in the new letters and banned teaching from books in the old ones [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
Old banknotes, shares and legal documents stayed valid until replaced [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

### 1932 onward: the language reform

TDK's own history gives the following account [@tdk_tarihce].
The Turkish Language Society was founded on 12 July 1932, on Atatürk's instruction, as [Türk Dili Tetkik Cemiyeti]{lang=tr} [@tdk_tarihce].
Its founders were four deputies and writers: Sâmih Rif'at, Ruşen Eşref, Celâl Sâhir and Yakup Kadri [@tdk_tarihce].
Its aim was to bring out the "[öz güzelliğini ve zenginliğini]{lang=tr}" (inherent beauty and richness) of Turkish [@tdk_tarihce].

According to TDK, a campaign to remove words of foreign origin ran until autumn 1935 [@tdk_tarihce].
After that, foreign-origin words already settled in everyday speech were kept [@tdk_tarihce].
The "[öz Türkçe]{lang=tr}" (pure Turkish) question stayed a debate among Turkish intellectuals after Atatürk's death [@tdk_tarihce].
Under Article 134 of the 1982 Constitution, the society was placed under a new state body [@tdk_tarihce].
That body is the [Atatürk Kültür, Dil ve Tarih Yüksek Kurumu]{lang=tr} (Atatürk High Institution of Culture, Language and History) [@tdk_tarihce].
An independent account of the reform is Geoffrey Lewis's book of 1999 [@lewis1999_reform]. TODO(verify): not opened; pages to cite.

The results show in today's dictionary.
TDK marks [mektep]{lang=tr} (from Arabic) as dated and sends the reader to [okul]{lang=tr}, "school" [@tdk_gts, s.v. mektep; s.v. okul].
It does the same for [tayyare]{lang=tr} (from Arabic), sending the reader to [uçak]{lang=tr}, "aeroplane" [@tdk_gts, s.v. tayyare].

## Sounds

Turkish has eight vowels: four front and four back [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Long [iː], [eː], [uː] and [aː] also occur [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Zimmer and Orgun illustrate them with a near-minimal set of short words, spoken by a 25-year-old from Istanbul [@zimmer_orgun1992]:

| Letter | IPA | Word | Gloss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [i]{lang=tr} | [i] | [kil]{lang=tr} [kil] | clay |
| [ü]{lang=tr} | [y] | [kül]{lang=tr} [kyl] | ashes |
| [e]{lang=tr} | [e] | [kel]{lang=tr} [kel] | bald |
| [ö]{lang=tr} | [œ] | [göl]{lang=tr} [ɡœl] | lake |
| [a]{lang=tr} | [a] | [kal]{lang=tr} [kaɫ] | stay |
| [ı]{lang=tr} | [ɯ] | [kıl]{lang=tr} [kɯɫ] | hair |
| [u]{lang=tr} | [u] | [kul]{lang=tr} [kuɫ] | slave |
| [o]{lang=tr} | [o] | [kol]{lang=tr} [koɫ] | arm |

Transcriptions as printed by the source, which writes k and g here [@zimmer_orgun1992].

Turkish has two l sounds: a palatalized [l] and a velarized [ɫ] [@zimmer_orgun1992].
[ɫ] never follows a front vowel, but [l] can appear with back vowels: [lale]{lang=tr} [laˈle] "tulip", [lala]{lang=tr} [ɫaˈɫa] "servant" [@zimmer_orgun1992].

### Vowel harmony

Suffix vowels agree with the vowel before them in backness [@utaustin_handout9; @halle_clements1983].
The plural has two forms, [-ler]{lang=tr} after front vowels and [-lar]{lang=tr} after back vowels [@utaustin_handout9; @halle_clements1983]:

| Singular | Plural | Gloss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [ip]{lang=tr} | [ipler]{lang=tr} | rope |
| [el]{lang=tr} | [eller]{lang=tr} | hand |
| [köy]{lang=tr} | [köyler]{lang=tr} | village |
| [yüz]{lang=tr} | [yüzler]{lang=tr} | face |
| [kız]{lang=tr} | [kızlar]{lang=tr} | girl |
| [pul]{lang=tr} | [pullar]{lang=tr} | stamp |

High-vowel suffixes also copy rounding, so they have four forms [@utaustin_handout9; @halle_clements1983].
The third-person possessive after a vowel shows this: [-sı, -si, -su, -sü]{lang=tr} [@utaustin_handout9; @halle_clements1983].

| Word | Its ... | Gloss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [oda]{lang=tr} | [odası]{lang=tr} | room |
| [dere]{lang=tr} | [deresi]{lang=tr} | river |
| [boru]{lang=tr} | [borusu]{lang=tr} | pipe |
| [ütü]{lang=tr} | [ütüsü]{lang=tr} | iron (for clothes) |

### Signature sounds

- **[ı]{lang=tr} [ɯ]:** a high back unrounded vowel, as in [kıl]{lang=tr} [kɯɫ] "hair" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **[ğ]{lang=tr}, "soft g":** it lengthens the vowel before it at the end of a word or before a consonant: [dağ]{lang=tr} [daː] "mountain" [@zimmer_orgun1992]. Between front vowels it is a weak palatal glide; between other vowels it is silent [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **[c]{lang=tr} and [ç]{lang=tr}:** [cam]{lang=tr} [dʒam] "glass" and [çam]{lang=tr} [tʃam] "pine" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **[ş]{lang=tr}:** [güneş]{lang=tr} [ɟyˈneʃ] "sun" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **[k]{lang=tr} and [g]{lang=tr} with front vowels:** in native words, [c] and [ɟ] appear with front vowels and [k] and [ɡ] with back vowels [@zimmer_orgun1992]. Some loanwords break the pattern: [kâr]{lang=tr} [caɾ] "profit" against [kar]{lang=tr} [kaɾ] "snow" [@zimmer_orgun1992].

### Stress

Stress usually falls on the last syllable [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Some suffixes cannot take stress; the stress then falls on the syllable just before them [@zimmer_orgun1992]:
[yaptırmalı]{lang=tr} [jap-tɯr-maˈɫɯ] "s/he must have (it) done", but [yaptırmamalı]{lang=tr} [jap-ˈtɯr-ma-maɫɯ] "s/he must refrain from having (it) done" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
Some words are exceptions, such as [masa]{lang=tr} [ˈmasa] "table" [@zimmer_orgun1992].

## Writing

Turkish, and the Turkic texts before it, have used several scripts.

- **Old Turkic script:** used on the Orkhon stones for an earlier Turkic language, not for Turkish itself [@kacalin2007_orhon].
- **Arabic script for Kashgari's Turkic headwords:** in the 11th-century [Dīwān]{lang=ar-Latn} [@kacalin1994_divan].
- **Arabic script:** the main script of Ottoman Turkish until 1928 [@ozkan2007_osmanli; @tr_kanun1353_1928].
- **Latin-based Turkish alphabet:** since the law of 1928 [@tr_kanun1353_1928].
- **Greek script:** TODO(verify) for Karamanli Turkish, which Glottolog lists as a Turkish variety [@glottolog53_nucl1301].
- **Armenian script:** TODO(verify) for Armeno-Turkish.

The 1928 table has 29 letters [@tr_kanun1353_1928]:

[A B C Ç D E F G Ğ H I İ J K L M N O Ö P R S Ş T U Ü V Y Z]{lang=tr}

It has no Q, W or X [@tr_kanun1353_1928].

### Four letters to know

- **[I ı]{lang=tr} and [İ i]{lang=tr}:** the table lists dotless [I ı]{lang=tr} and dotted [İ i]{lang=tr} as two separate letter pairs [@tr_kanun1353_1928]. Dotless [ı]{lang=tr} is [ɯ]; dotted [i]{lang=tr} is [i] [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **[Ş ş]{lang=tr}:** [ʃ], as in [güneş]{lang=tr} [ɟyˈneʃ] "sun" [@zimmer_orgun1992].
- **[Ğ ğ]{lang=tr}:** lengthens the vowel before it at the end of a word or before a consonant; between front vowels it is a weak palatal glide; between other vowels it is silent [@zimmer_orgun1992].

The dotless and dotted pairs matter for software.
Unicode's special casing rules for Turkish map lowercase [i]{lang=tr} to uppercase [İ]{lang=tr}, and uppercase [I]{lang=tr} to lowercase [ı]{lang=tr} [@unicode_specialcasing].

## Grammar DNA

1. **Vowel harmony.** Suffix vowels match the stem: [kızlar]{lang=tr} "girls", [köyler]{lang=tr} "villages" [@utaustin_handout9; @halle_clements1983].

2. **Almost all suffixes.** WALS classes Turkish's inflection as strongly suffixing [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 26A].
   [yap-tır-malı]{lang=tr} (do-CAUS-NEC) "s/he must have (it) done" [@zimmer_orgun1992]. Gloss labels: TODO(verify).

3. **The evidential [-miş]{lang=tr}.** Turkish marks whether the speaker witnessed the event or knows it from inference or hearsay [@dehaan2013_wals77].
   [Ahmet gel-di]{lang=tr} (Ahmet come-PST.DIR.EVD) "Ahmet came" (witnessed) [@dehaan2013_wals77].
   [Ahmet gel-miş]{lang=tr} (Ahmet come-PST.INDIR.EVD) "Ahmet came" (not witnessed) [@dehaan2013_wals77].

4. **Verb last.** WALS records subject, object, verb order [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 81A].
   From the North Wind and the Sun: [Derken, kalın bir palto giymiş bir yolcu gördüler]{lang=tr} [@zimmer_orgun1992].
   "Then they saw a traveller wearing a thick coat" (our translation).

5. **No grammatical gender.** WALS records no gender system [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 30A].
   Example: TODO(verify), a sourced pronoun paradigm.

6. **No definite article; [bir]{lang=tr} for "a".** WALS records an indefinite article but no definite one [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 37A].
   [kalın bir palto]{lang=tr} "a thick coat" [@zimmer_orgun1992].

7. **Postpositions.** Words like "for", "like" and "with" follow their noun [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 85A].
   Example: TODO(verify), a sourced sentence with [için]{lang=tr}, [gibi]{lang=tr} or [ile]{lang=tr} [@goksel_kerslake2005]. The grammar was not opened.

8. **Question particle.** Yes-or-no questions use a particle [@wals2013_trfeat, feature 116A].
   Example: TODO(verify), a sourced sentence with [mi]{lang=tr}.

9. **Stress that suffixes can move.** Negative [-ma]{lang=tr} cannot take stress, so stress falls on the syllable before it [@zimmer_orgun1992].
   [yaptırmalı]{lang=tr} [jap-tɯr-maˈɫɯ] against [yaptırmamalı]{lang=tr} [jap-ˈtɯr-ma-maɫɯ] [@zimmer_orgun1992].

10. **Consonants that change before vowels.** In many words a final [p]{lang=tr}, [t]{lang=tr}, [ç]{lang=tr} or [k]{lang=tr} turns voiced before a vowel suffix [@utaustin_handout9; @halle_clements1983].
    [kap]{lang=tr} "container", [kaba]{lang=tr} "to the container"; [renk]{lang=tr} "colour", [renge]{lang=tr} "to the colour" [@utaustin_handout9].
    Other words keep it: [sap]{lang=tr} "stalk", [sapa]{lang=tr} "to the stalk" [@utaustin_handout9].

11. **Long derived words.** Causatives and other suffixes can pile up inside one word. TODO(verify): source the morphology to Göksel and Kerslake [@goksel_kerslake2005].
    [paltosunu çıkarttırabilenin]{lang=tr}, from the North Wind passage [@zimmer_orgun1992]: "whoever could make him take off his coat", with the genitive marking the subject of the next clause (our gloss). Segmentation: TODO(verify).

## Connections

### Family

Glottolog puts Turkish in the Turkic family, Oghuz branch, West Oghuz group [@glottolog53_nucl1301].
South Azerbaijani is also Oghuz, in Glottolog's Nuclear Oghuz group, under Azerbaijani rather than West Oghuz [@glottolog53_sout2697].
Other Turkic languages in this atlas include Northern Uzbek and Kazakh. The Family tab shows where each sits.

### Contact

- **Persian:** everyday words such as [pencere]{lang=tr}, [hafta]{lang=tr}, [şehir]{lang=tr} and [dost]{lang=tr} [@tdk_gts]. Ottoman writers also used Persian compounds [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
- **Arabic:** [kitap]{lang=tr}, [kalem]{lang=tr}, [mektep]{lang=tr} and [tayyare]{lang=tr} [@tdk_gts]. Ottoman Turkish used Arabic derivations and plurals [@ozkan2007_osmanli].
- **Greek:** TDK traces [fener]{lang=tr} and [liman]{lang=tr} to [Rumca]{lang=tr}, the Greek of Anatolia and Istanbul [@tdk_gts, s.v. fener; s.v. liman]. TODO(verify): the gloss of "[Rumca]{lang=tr}".
- **French:** [otobüs]{lang=tr}, from [autobus]{lang=fr} [@tdk_gts, s.v. otobüs].

### A contested deeper family

An older proposal called "Altaic" grouped Turkic with Mongolic and Tungusic; WALS still files Turkish under "Altaic" [@wals2013_tur]. TODO(verify): a source for the scope of classic Altaic.
Some scholars now group Turkic with Mongolic, Tungusic, Koreanic and Japonic as "Transeurasian" [@robbeets2021].
Robbeets and colleagues argued in 2021 that the first farmers of Northeast Asia spread these languages [@robbeets2021].
Tian and colleagues replied in a 2022 preprint, not peer-reviewed, that the evidence supports neither the family nor that spread [@tian2022].
Robbeets and colleagues answered in another preprint [@robbeets2022_reply].
Most historical linguists explain the shared features by contact. TODO(verify): Vovin 2005 (Central Asiatic Journal 49(1): 71 to 132), not opened.
Glottolog treats Turkic as an independent family [@glottolog53_nucl1301].
This atlas shows the question as contested.

## Oddity: whistled Turkish

In the steep hills of Türkiye's eastern Black Sea region, some farming families talk across valleys by whistling [@unesco_ich_whistled; @unesco_ich_whistled_nomination].
UNESCO inscribed "Whistled language" of Türkiye on its Urgent Safeguarding List in 2017 [@unesco_ich_whistled].
The nomination names Kuşköy and Karabörk, villages in the Çanakçı district of Giresun, as places where it is widely practised [@unesco_ich_whistled_nomination].
Giresun hosts a Bird Language Festival [@unesco_ich_whistled_nomination]. TODO(verify): the Turkish name of the festival and of the practice ([kuş dili]{lang=tr}).

The whistles carry spoken Turkish, with the same vocabulary and grammar, over several kilometres [@eurekalert2015_whistled].
UNESCO names mobile phones and fading interest among young people as the main threats [@unesco_ich_whistled].

In 2015 a team from Ruhr-Universität Bochum tested 31 people from Kuşköy [@eurekalert2015_whistled; @gunturkun2015_whistled].
For spoken syllables they found the usual left-hemisphere advantage; for whistled syllables it disappeared [@eurekalert2015_whistled; @gunturkun2015_whistled].
These details come from the university's press release; the article's full text was not opened.

- **The Orkhon language and Oghuz.** How close is the language of the stones to the ancestor of Turkish? TODO(verify): a citable summary.

- **When does Ottoman begin?** Özkan starts it in the 13th century; others start it after an Old Anatolian Turkish stage [@ozkan2007_osmanli]. TODO(verify): Kerslake 1998.
- **How pure should Turkish be?** The [öz Türkçe]{lang=tr} debate continued after 1938 [@tdk_tarihce].
