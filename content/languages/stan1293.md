# English

## Hook

The everyday pronoun *they* is a borrowing from Old Norse, the language of Scandinavian settlers in medieval England [@wiktionary_they; @dhi_gersum; @dhi_gersum_followon].

## Story

### Old English: Beowulf

Glottolog names this stage "Old English (ca. 450-1100)" [@glottolog53_olde1238].

*Beowulf* survives in one manuscript, British Library Cotton MS Vitellius A XV [@mcmaster_beowulf_ms; @bl2013_beowulf_online].

The British Library dates the manuscript to around the year 1000 [@bl2013_beowulf_online].

A fire damaged it in 1731, yet much of the text can still be read [@bl2013_beowulf_online].

The poem opens on folio 132r with the word *Hwæt* [@bl2013_beowulf_online].

The text below is from the edition by James A. Harrison and Robert Sharp (4th edition, Ginn, 1893) [@harrison_sharp1893_beowulf].

That edition follows M. Heyne's fourth edition (Paderborn, 1879). Here are the first three lines [@harrison_sharp1893_beowulf]:

> Hwæt! wē Gār-Dena in geār-dagum
> þēod-cyninga þrym gefrūnon,
> hū þā æðelingas ellen fremedon.

Working translation (the writer's own, for review): "Listen! We have heard of the glory of the people-kings of the Spear-Danes in days of old, how those princes did deeds of courage." TODO(verify) translation.

Look at the letters þ, ð and æ. They return in the Writing section.

### Middle English: Chaucer

Glottolog lists Middle English as its own languoid, with ISO 639-3 code `enm` [@glottolog53_midd1317].

French was written in England in this period. The Anglo-Norman Dictionary records *boef* "ox" from 1113-19, and in the sense "beef" from 1312-13 [@and_boef].

Geoffrey Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* opens with this sentence. The text is from Walter W. Skeat's edition (Clarendon Press, 2nd edition, 1900) [@skeat1900_chaucer4]:

> Whan that Aprille with his shoures sote
> The droghte of Marche hath perced to the rote,
> And bathed every veyne in swich licour,
> Of which vertu engendred is the flour;

Working translation (the writer's own): "When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root, and bathed every vein in such liquid, by whose power the flower is brought forth." TODO(verify) translation.

Note *his* where modern English has *its*, and *sote* for "sweet".

### Early Modern English: Shakespeare

The First Folio of Shakespeare's plays was printed in 1623 by Isaac Jaggard and Edward Blount [@folio1623_pg2270].

Project Gutenberg's transcription is a composite of more than 30 Folio copies, with long s and ligatures modernized [@folio1623_pg2270].

Hamlet's soliloquy begins like this in that text [@folio1623_pg2270]:

> To be, or not to be, that is the Question:
> Whether 'tis Nobler in the minde to suffer
> The Slings and Arrowes of outragious Fortune,
> Or to take Armes against a Sea of troubles,

Modern spelling: "To be, or not to be, that is the question: whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune, or to take arms against a sea of troubles."

The words are nearly modern. The spelling (*minde*, *Arrowes*, *outragious*) is not yet fixed.

**Original Pronunciation is a reconstruction.** In 2004 Shakespeare's Globe staged *Romeo and Juliet* in a reconstructed accent [@crystal2005_pronouncing].

The linguist David Crystal told the story of that production in *Pronouncing Shakespeare* (2005) [@crystal2005_pronouncing].

The reconstruction draws on spelling variants, comments by writers of the period and the deductions of historical linguists [@op_site].

These sources do not always agree [@op_site].

The project's own site says the work "inevitably involves a certain amount of guesswork" [@op_site].

It also stresses that there is no single Original Pronunciation, because accents varied then as now [@op_site].

Any audio for this stage carries the Reconstruction badge and names its scholars.

### The 18th century: Johnson's Dictionary

Samuel Johnson's *Dictionary of the English Language* was first published in London in 1755 [@patkus_vassar_johnson].

Vassar College holds two copies of that first London edition [@patkus_vassar_johnson].

In his Preface, Johnson doubted that any authority could freeze a language. This sample is from Project Gutenberg's text, which does not name its print source [@johnson1755_preface_pg5430]:

> academies have been instituted, to guard the avenues of their languages, to retain fugitives, and repulse intruders;

He went on to call the wish "to enchain syllables, and to lash the wind" an undertaking of pride [@johnson1755_preface_pg5430].

### The Great Vowel Shift: a scholarly account

Scholars use this name for a chain of changes to the long vowels of Middle English [@prichard2014_gvs].

The usual description, as summarized by Hilary Prichard (2014), includes these steps [@prichard2014_gvs]:

| Middle English vowel | Later outcome | Example words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ī | [aɪ] | *time*, *knife* |
| ē | [iː] | *three*, *sheep* |
| ɛ̄ | [iː] | *team*, *eat* |
| ā | [ɛː] > [eː] > [eɪ] | *spade*, *bacon* |
| ū | diphthongized | *house*, *clouds* |
| ō | raised | *goose*, *moon* |

The steps took centuries. Prichard's summary has the raising of ā beginning in the 15th century and modern [eɪ] arriving around 1800 [@prichard2014_gvs].

**What scholars debate.**

Order: Otto Jespersen proposed a drag chain, where the high vowels moved first and pulled the others up [@prichard2014_gvs].

Karl Luick proposed a push chain, where the mid vowels rose first and pushed the high vowels out [@prichard2014_gvs].

Luick's case leaned on the North. There, Middle English ū never became a diphthong, so *house* kept its old vowel [@prichard2014_gvs].

Unity: Robert Stockwell and Donka Minkova (1988) argued that dialects did not all follow the same path, so the "shift" may be several changes [@prichard2014_gvs].

They called it "the linguist's creation through hindsight", as quoted by Prichard [@prichard2014_gvs].

Prichard's own study of Northern dialect maps supports Luick's push chain. She explains the irregular patterns as later spread from the south [@prichard2014_gvs].

The animated sound-change rule planned for this page must show both chain models, labeled as hypotheses.

### Loanwords in

**From Old Norse.** The Gersum project (Cambridge and Cardiff) catalogued English words for which a Norse origin has been suggested [@asnc2016_gersum; @caths2021_gersum].

Its examples include *sky*, *egg*, *law*, *leg*, *take*, *window*, *knife*, *die*, *skin* and *they* [@dhi_gersum].

The project notes that these are mostly everyday words, such as *husband*, *ugly* and *scare* [@dhi_gersum_followon].

The project takes its name from Middle English *gersum* "treasure", borrowed from Old Norse *gørsemi* [@asnc2016_gersum].

*they*: Middle English *þei*, from Old Norse *þeir*. It replaced Old English *hīe*, which had become hard to tell apart from *he* [@wiktionary_they]. TODO(verify) against the Middle English Dictionary or OED, which refused automated access.

**From French.** English *beef* matches Anglo-Norman *boef*, which meant both "ox" and "beef" [@and_boef].

English *pork* matches Anglo-Norman *porc*, "pig" and "pork" [@and_porc].

A 12th-century Latin gloss in the Anglo-Norman Dictionary pairs the two languages: "porc vel swyn", that is, *porc* or *swine* [@and_porc].

**From Latin.** Old English already had *biscop* "bishop". Bosworth-Toller glosses it with Latin *episcopus* [@bt_biscop]. TODO(verify) a source that states the borrowing path.

### Loanwords out

French *week-end* is a borrowing from English [@tlfi_weekend].

The Trésor de la langue française gives a first example from 1906, describing England, and a first example for France from 1926 [@tlfi_weekend].

### False friends

**German *Handy*.** In German it means a mobile phone [@dwds_handy].

DWDS classes it as a pseudo-anglicism, because English speakers do not use *handy* for a phone [@dwds_handy].

**German *Gift*.** In German it means "poison" [@dwds_gift].

It comes from the verb *geben* "give". The old sense "gift" survives in *Mitgift* "dowry" [@dwds_gift].

DWDS cites Old English *gift* as a related form [@dwds_gift].

## Writing

**Now:** the Latin alphabet.

**Before:** runes, beside the Latin alphabet. The Franks Casket, a carved box of about 700 to 750, is in the British Museum [@gac_franks_casket].

Its inscriptions use runic and Roman letters, in Old English and in Latin [@gac_franks_casket].

Its front panel holds a runic riddle about a whale washed ashore, the source of the bone [@gac_franks_casket].

**Old English letters.** Manuscripts write *þ* (thorn) and *ð* (eth) where modern English writes *th* [@snsbi_oe_pronunciation].

The two letters were "used indiscriminately" for the sounds in *father* and *thin* [@snsbi_oe_pronunciation].

*Æ æ* (ash) stood for a vowel like the one in *hat* [@snsbi_oe_pronunciation].

All three appear in the Beowulf lines above: *þēod*, *æðelingas*, *Hwæt* [@harrison_sharp1893_beowulf].

### Spelling reforms that did and did not happen

**Did happen, in the United States.** Noah Webster's dictionaries of 1806 and 1828 promoted *honor*, *center*, *plow* and *wagon* [@mencken1919].

**Did not happen.** Webster's 1806 dictionary also printed *wimmen*, *iland* and *thum*. His 1828 dictionary went back to the usual forms [@mencken1919].

In 1906 President Theodore Roosevelt ordered government printing to use simplified spellings of three hundred words from the Simplified Spelling Board [@trc_roosevelt_stillings1906].

The House of Representatives rejected the order, and the reform was not adopted for good [@trc_spelling_reform].

## Connections

**Closest relatives.** Glottolog puts English in the North Sea Germanic and Anglo-Frisian branches of West Germanic [@glottolog53_stan1293].

Dutch is also West Germanic, but on a separate branch [@glottolog53_dutc1256].

**Old English as a sister, not a parent.** In Glottolog's tree, the Old English languoid sits beside the branch that leads to English [@glottolog53_olde1238; @glottolog53_stan1293].

That branch, "Middle-Modern English", has the alternative name Mercian [@glottolog53_merc1242].

**Contact with Norse.** Glottolog places Swedish in North Germanic [@glottolog53_swed1254]. Old Norse in the same branch: TODO(verify) its Glottolog node.

Norse settlement in England is dated to the 9th to 11th centuries on the Gersum project's page [@dhi_gersum_followon].

**Contact with French.** Anglo-Norman records show the two languages side by side in England from the 12th century [@and_porc; @and_boef].

**Contact with Latin.** Old English church vocabulary is glossed with Latin in Bosworth-Toller; see *biscop* above [@bt_biscop]. Routes of Latin borrowing: TODO(verify).

**English as a source.** Glottolog files Nigerian Pidgin under Macro-English, the branch that also holds English [@glottolog53_nige1257; @glottolog53_stan1293].

## Open questions

1. **Was the Great Vowel Shift one event?** Stockwell and Minkova say several changes; Prichard defends a single push chain [@prichard2014_gvs].
2. **Push or drag?** Luick and Jespersen disagreed on which vowels moved first [@prichard2014_gvs].
3. **How sure is Original Pronunciation?** Its own practitioners call it partly guesswork and plural [@op_site].
4. **How many Norse words?** Gersum pages give "up to 1600" suggested words and "more than 900" traced words [@asnc2016_gersum; @caths2021_gersum].
5. **Which Old English is the ancestor?** Glottolog does not place the Old English languoid on the direct line to English [@glottolog53_olde1238; @glottolog53_merc1242]. Other classifications: TODO(verify).
