### Old English: Beowulf

Glottolog names this stage "Old English (ca. 450-1100)" [@glottolog53_olde1238].

*Beowulf* survives in one medieval manuscript, British Library Cotton MS Vitellius A XV [@bl_cotton_vitellius_axv].

The British Library dates that part of the volume to the very end of the 10th century or, more likely, the early 11th [@bl_cotton_vitellius_axv].

When the poem itself was composed is contested. Proposals run from the 8th century to the time of the manuscript [@neidorf2014]. TODO(verify), Neidorf 2014 seen in catalog only.

The poem opens on folio 132r with the word *[Hwæt]{lang=ang}*, traditionally read as "Listen!" [@bl2013_beowulf_online]. Walkden (2013) re-examines what *[hwæt]{lang=ang}* does [@walkden2013_hwaet]. TODO(verify), abstract only.

The text below is from the edition by James A. Harrison and Robert Sharp (4th edition, Ginn, 1893) [@harrison_sharp1893_beowulf].

That edition follows M. Heyne's fourth edition (Paderborn, 1879). Here are the first three lines [@harrison_sharp1893_beowulf]:

> [Hwæt! wē Gār-Dena in geār-dagum]{lang=ang}
> [þēod-cyninga þrym gefrūnon,]{lang=ang}
> [hū þā æðelingas ellen fremedon.]{lang=ang}

Working translation (the writer's own, for review): "Listen! We have heard of the glory of the people-kings of the Spear-Danes in days of old, how those princes did deeds of courage." TODO(verify) translation.

Look at the letters þ, ð and æ. They return in the Writing section.

### Middle English: Chaucer

Glottolog lists Middle English as its own languoid, with ISO 639-3 code `enm` [@glottolog53_midd1317].

French was written in England in this period. The Anglo-Norman Dictionary dates its headword *[boef]{lang=xno}* "ox" to 1113 to 1119 [@and_boef].

The sense "beef" appears from the early 14th century, including a dated account of 1312 to 1313 [@and_boef].

The Norman Conquest of 1066 as the start of this contact, and the scale of French borrowing into Middle English: TODO(verify), no source opened yet.

Geoffrey Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* opens with this sentence. The text is from Walter W. Skeat's edition (Clarendon Press, 2nd edition, 1900) [@skeat1900_chaucer4]:

> [Whan that Aprille with his shoures sote]{lang=enm}
> [The droghte of Marche hath perced to the rote,]{lang=enm}
> [And bathed every veyne in swich licour,]{lang=enm}
> [Of which vertu engendred is the flour;]{lang=enm}

Working translation (the writer's own): "When April with its sweet showers has pierced the drought of March to the root, and bathed every vein in such liquid, by whose power the flower is brought forth." TODO(verify) translation.

Note *[his]{lang=enm}* where modern English has *its*, and *[sote]{lang=enm}* for "sweet".

### Early Modern English: Shakespeare

The First Folio of Shakespeare's plays was printed in 1623 by Isaac Jaggard and Edward Blount [@folio1623_pg2270].

Project Gutenberg's transcription is a composite of more than 30 Folio copies, with long s and ligatures modernized [@folio1623_pg2270].

Hamlet's soliloquy begins like this in that text [@folio1623_pg2270]:

> To be, or not to be, that is the Question:
> Whether 'tis Nobler in the minde to suffer
> The Slings and Arrowes of outragious Fortune,
> Or to take Armes against a Sea of troubles,

Modern spelling: "To be, or not to be, that is the question: whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune, or to take arms against a sea of troubles."

The words are nearly modern. The spelling (*minde*, *Arrowes*, *outragious*) is not yet fixed.

**Original Pronunciation is a reconstruction.** In 2004 Shakespeare's Globe staged *Romeo and Juliet* in a reconstructed accent [@crystal2005_pronouncing].

The linguist David Crystal told the story of that production in *Pronouncing Shakespeare: The Globe Experiment* (2005) [@crystal2005_pronouncing].

Reconstructions of this kind draw on spelling variants, comments by writers of the period and the deductions of historical linguists [@op_site].

These sources do not always agree [@op_site].

The site originalpronunciation.com says the work "inevitably involves a certain amount of guesswork" [@op_site]. Who runs the site: TODO(verify).

It also stresses that there is no single Original Pronunciation, because accents varied then as now [@op_site].

WALS describes them as dental or alveolar fricatives that are not sibilants, and uses English *thing* and *that* as reference points [@wals_ch19].

**Tone.** English has no lexical tone in WALS [@wals_eng_features]. Mandarin, by contrast, has a complex tone system [@wals_mnd_13A].

### The Great Vowel Shift: a scholarly account

Scholars use this name for a chain of changes to the long vowels of Middle English [@prichard2014_gvs].

The Middle English values are themselves reconstructions, from spelling, rhyme and 16th- and 17th-century writers on pronunciation [@prichard2014_gvs]. TODO(verify) this sentence against a handbook.

The usual description, as summarized by Hilary Prichard (2014), includes these steps [@prichard2014_gvs]:

| Middle English vowel (IPA) | Later outcome | Example words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /iː/ (ī) | [aɪ] | *time*, *knife* |
| /eː/ (ē) | [iː] | *three*, *sheep* |
| /ɛː/ (ɛ̄) | [iː] | *team*, *eat* |
| /aː/ (ā) | [ɛː] > [eː] > [eɪ] | *spade*, *bacon* |
| /uː/ (ū) | diphthongized | *house*, *clouds* |
| /oː/ (ō) | raised | *goose*, *moon* |
| /ɔː/ (ɔ̄) | raised, later [oʊ] | *goat*. TODO(verify) |

The steps took centuries. Prichard's summary has the raising of ā beginning in the 15th century and modern [eɪ] arriving around 1800 [@prichard2014_gvs].

**What scholars debate.**

Order: Otto Jespersen proposed a drag chain, where the high vowels moved first and pulled the others up [@prichard2014_gvs].

Karl Luick proposed a push chain, where the mid vowels rose first and pushed the high vowels out [@prichard2014_gvs].

Luick's case leaned on the North. There, Middle English ū never became a diphthong, so *house* kept its old vowel [@prichard2014_gvs].

Unity: Robert Stockwell and Donka Minkova (1988) argued that dialects did not all follow the same path, so the "shift" may be several changes [@stockwell_minkova1988; @prichard2014_gvs].

They called it "the linguist's creation through hindsight", as quoted by Prichard [@prichard2014_gvs].

Prichard's own study of Northern dialect maps supports Luick's push chain. She explains the irregular patterns as later spread from the south [@prichard2014_gvs].

### Loanwords in

**From Old Norse.** The Gersum project (Cambridge and Cardiff) catalogued words of possible Norse origin in major Middle English poems from the North of England, such as *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* [@dhi_gersum].

Its examples include *sky*, *egg*, *law*, *leg*, *take*, *window*, *knife*, *die* and *skin* [@dhi_gersum].

The project notes that these are mostly everyday words, such as *husband*, *ugly* and *scare* [@dhi_gersum_followon].

The project takes its name from Middle English *[gersum]{lang=enm}* "treasure", borrowed from Old Norse *[gørsemi]{lang=non}* [@asnc2016_gersum].

**The case of *they*.** Most accounts derive Middle English *[þei]{lang=enm}* from Old Norse *[þeir]{lang=non}*, replacing Old English *[hīe]{lang=ang}* [@cole2018_they; @wiktionary_they].

A common explanation is that *[hīe]{lang=ang}* had become hard to tell apart from *he* [@wiktionary_they].

Marcelle Cole (2018) argues instead for a native source: the Old English demonstrative forms *[þā]{lang=ang}*, *[þāra]{lang=ang}*, *[þām]{lang=ang}*, reanalysed as personal pronouns in the North [@cole2018_they].

TODO(verify) the etymology against the Middle English Dictionary and the OED, which refused automated access.

**From French.** English *beef* matches Anglo-Norman *[boef]{lang=xno}*, which meant both "ox" and "beef" [@and_boef].

English *pork* matches Anglo-Norman *[porc]{lang=xno}*, "pig" and "pork" [@and_porc].

A manuscript of the early 12th century glosses Latin *[sus]{lang=la}* as "[porc]{lang=xno} [vel]{lang=la} [swyn]{lang=enm}": French *[porc]{lang=xno}* or English *swine* [@and_porc].

**From Latin.** Old English already had *[biscop]{lang=ang}* "bishop". Bosworth-Toller derives it from Latin *[episcopus]{lang=la}*, from Greek *[epískopos]{lang=grc-Latn}* "overseer" [@bt_biscop].

### Loanwords out

French *[week-end]{lang=fr}* is a borrowing from English [@tlfi_weekend].

The [Trésor de la langue française]{lang=fr} gives a first example from 1906, describing England, and a first example for France from 1926 [@tlfi_weekend].

### False friends

**German *[Handy]{lang=de}*.** In German it means a mobile phone [@dwds_handy].

DWDS classes it as a pseudo-anglicism, because English speakers do not use *handy* for a phone [@dwds_handy].

**German *[Gift]{lang=de}*.** In German it means "poison" [@dwds_gift].

It comes from the verb *[geben]{lang=de}* "give". The old sense "gift" survives in *[Mitgift]{lang=de}* "dowry" [@dwds_gift].

DWDS cites Old English *[gift]{lang=ang}* as a related form [@dwds_gift].

## Writing

**Now:** the Latin alphabet.

**Before:** runes, beside the Latin alphabet. The Franks Casket, a carved box of about 700 to 750, is in the British Museum [@gac_franks_casket].

Its inscriptions use runic and Roman letters, in Old English and in Latin [@gac_franks_casket].

Its front panel holds a runic riddle about a whale washed ashore, the source of the bone [@gac_franks_casket].

**Old English letters.** Manuscripts write *þ* (thorn) and *ð* (eth) where modern English writes *th* [@snsbi_oe_pronunciation].

The two letters were "used indiscriminately" for the sounds in *father* and *thin* [@snsbi_oe_pronunciation].

*Æ æ* (ash) stood for a vowel like the one in *hat* [@snsbi_oe_pronunciation].

All three appear in the Beowulf lines above: *[þēod]{lang=ang}*, *[æðelingas]{lang=ang}*, *[Hwæt]{lang=ang}* [@harrison_sharp1893_beowulf].

Thorn's origin as a rune, and wynn (ƿ) for /w/: TODO(verify).

### Spelling reforms that did and did not happen

**Did happen, in the United States.** Noah Webster's dictionaries of 1806 and 1828 promoted *honor*, *center*, *plow* and *wagon* [@mencken1919].

**Did not happen.** Webster's 1806 dictionary also printed *wimmen*, *iland* and *thum*. His 1828 dictionary went back to the usual forms [@mencken1919].

In 1906 President Theodore Roosevelt ordered government printing to use simplified spellings of three hundred words from the Simplified Spelling Board [@trc_roosevelt_stillings1906].

The House of Representatives rejected the order, and the reform was not adopted for good [@trc_spelling_reform].

## Grammar DNA

Values come from WALS [@wals_eng_features]. The examples are the writer's.

1. **Subject, verb, object** (81A, SVO): *The dog bit the man.*
2. **Prepositions** (85A): *in the house*, not "the house in".
3. **Adjective before noun** (87A): *a red door*.
4. **A definite article distinct from "that"** (37A): *the book* versus *that book*.
5. **An indefinite article distinct from "one"** (38A): *a book* versus *one book*.
6. **Plural suffix** (33A): *cat*, *cats*.
7. **Two cases** (49A): WALS counts two cases on nouns, which points to a plain form and the genitive, as in *the dog's* [@wals_eng_features; @wals_ch49]. TODO(verify) against a reference grammar; the chapter does not discuss English.
8. **Three genders** (30A): WALS counts English because its pronouns *he*, *she*, *it* show gender, the best-known case of this kind [@wals_ch30].
9. **No inflected future** (67A): *I will go*, built with a helper verb.
10. **Subject pronouns required** (101A): *She sings*, never just "sings".
11. **Questions by word order** (116A): *You are ready* becomes *Are you ready?*

## Connections

**Closest relatives.** Glottolog puts English in the North Sea Germanic and Anglo-Frisian branches of West Germanic [@glottolog53_stan1293].

Glottolog's nearest relative of Anglic is Frisian [@glottolog53_angl1264].

Whether English and Frisian share a common Anglo-Frisian ancestor is debated [@stiles1995_anglofrisian]. TODO(verify), Stiles 1995 not opened.

Dutch is also West Germanic, but on a separate branch [@glottolog53_dutc1256].

**How Glottolog draws stages.** Glottolog files attested older stages beside the line, not on it [@glottolog53_angl1265; @glottolog53_late1254].

Old English sits next to Later Anglic, the branch that holds Scots and Middle-Modern English [@glottolog53_angl1265; @glottolog53_late1254].

Middle English sits next to Macro-English [@glottolog53_merc1242; @glottolog53_midd1317].

The reason is dialect. Most surviving Old English is West Saxon. Modern standard English grew mainly from Midland speech of Mercian, or Anglian, origin [@baughcable2013]. TODO(verify), page not opened.

Middle-Modern English carries the alternative name Mercian in Glottolog [@glottolog53_merc1242].

Handbooks still treat Old English as the earliest stage of English [@hogg1992]. TODO(verify), not opened.

**Contact with Norse.** Glottolog places Old Norse in North Germanic, under West Scandinavian; Swedish is East Scandinavian [@glottolog53_oldn1244; @glottolog53_swed1254].

Much of the Norse spoken in the Danelaw may have been East Norse; "Old Norse" is the conventional cover term. TODO(verify), no source yet.

Scandinavian settlement in Britain, 9th to 11th centuries, is noted on the Gersum project's page [@dhi_gersum_followon].

**Contact with French.** Anglo-Norman records show the two languages side by side in England from the 12th century [@and_porc; @and_boef].

**Contact with Latin.** Old English church words such as *[biscop]{lang=ang}* came from Latin [@bt_biscop]. Other routes of Latin borrowing: TODO(verify).

**Pidgins and creoles.** Glottolog classifies Nigerian Pidgin under Macro-English, by its main word source [@glottolog53_nige1257].

Whether creoles belong to their lexifier's family is debated. TODO(verify), no source opened. Nigerian Pidgin's endonym ([Naijá]{lang=pcm}): TODO(verify).

## Open questions

1. **Was the Great Vowel Shift one event?** Stockwell and Minkova say several changes; Prichard defends a single push chain [@stockwell_minkova1988; @prichard2014_gvs].
2. **Push or drag?** Luick and Jespersen disagreed on which vowels moved first [@prichard2014_gvs].
3. **Where did *they* come from?** Old Norse borrowing is the common view; Cole (2018) argues for a native Old English source [@cole2018_they].
4. **How sure is Original Pronunciation?** Its practitioners call it partly guesswork, and say there is more than one [@op_site].
5. **How many Norse words?** In the Gersum corpus, project pages give "up to 1600" candidate words (2016) and "more than 900" examined (2021) [@asnc2016_gersum; @caths2021_gersum]. The project dates also differ: 2016 to 2019 on the DHI page, 2016 to 2020 at St Catharine's [@dhi_gersum; @caths2021_gersum].
6. **When was *Beowulf* composed?** Proposals run from the 8th century to about 1000 [@neidorf2014]. TODO(verify).
7. **Which Old English is the ancestor?** Surviving Old English is mostly West Saxon, while modern standard English grew mainly from Mercian-origin Midland speech; Glottolog therefore files Old English beside the line [@glottolog53_angl1265; @baughcable2013]. TODO(verify) Baugh and Cable pages.
8. **Do creoles belong to their lexifier's family?** Glottolog files Nigerian Pidgin under Macro-English [@glottolog53_nige1257]. Other views: TODO(verify).
