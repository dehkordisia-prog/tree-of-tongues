# Iranian Persian

## Hook

English "paradise" goes back to an old Iranian word for a walled enclosure [@wikt-paridayjah; @wikt-paradise, English etymology].

## At a glance

- **English name:** Iranian Persian. Other English names include Persian, New Persian and Tehrani Persian [@glottolog-west2369, alternative names].
- **Endonym:** فارسی (fārsi). IPA (Iran, formal): [fɒːɹ.síː] [@wikt-fa-endonym]. Tertiary source, medium confidence.
- **Family path (Glottolog 5.3):** Indo-European → Classical Indo-European → Indo-Iranian → Iranian-Nuristani → Iranian → Southwestern Iranian → Middle-Modern Persian → Modern Southwestern Iranian → Farsic-Caucasian Tat → Farsic → Iranian Persian [@glottolog53, languages.csv#west2369].
- **A note on the Farsic nodes:** their English display labels are an open owner decision. The node Farsic-Caucasian Tat also contains Caucasian Tat, so this atlas never labels it "Persian" [@glottolog53, languages.csv#fars1254].
- **Codes:** ISO 639-3 pes; Glottocode west2369 [@glottolog-west2369].
- **Script:** Perso-Arabic [@wp-persian-language].
- **Status:** Glottolog rates it not endangered [@glottolog-west2369, comments]. Article 15 of Iran's Constitution (1979, revised 1989) names Persian the official language and script [@constitute-iran1989]. ## Persian as one pluricentric language

*Atlas grouping, provisional: the owner has not yet decided this framing.*

Persian is often described as one language with three standard forms [@wp-persian-language, lead; @windfuhr_perry2009]. TODO(verify): page locator in Windfuhr and Perry; the chapter was not opened.

Iran's standard is the subject of this page. Afghanistan's is called Dari, and Tajikistan's is called Tajik [@wp-persian-language, lead].

The three standards are usually described as mutually intelligible, especially in formal use [@wp-persian-language, lead; @wp-dari].

Iranian Persian and Dari are written in Perso-Arabic script. Tajik is written in Cyrillic [@wp-persian-language; @wp-tajik-alphabet, lead].

The code books split them differently. ISO 639-3 has a macrolanguage, fas, that covers Iranian Persian (pes) and Dari (prs) only [@wp-iso-macrolanguage, table row fas].

Tajik (tgk) stands outside that macrolanguage [@wp-iso-macrolanguage, table row fas]. TODO(verify): confirm both points in SIL's ISO 639-3 tables; the SIL site refused automated requests.

Glottolog draws its own tree. Iranian Persian sits under a node called Farsic. Dari and Tajik sit under Eastern Farsic, and Tajik in a further subgroup, Tajikic [@glottolog53, languages.csv#dari1249, #taji1245].

This atlas's Persian lens groups all three standards. That is our grouping, not ISO's.

It follows scholars who treat Tajik as a form of Persian. John R. Perry titled his reference grammar *A Tajik Persian Reference Grammar* [@perry2005].

Glottolog cites the chapter "Persian and Tajik" by Gernot Windfuhr and John R. Perry (2009), pages 417 to 421, for this part of its tree [@glottolog-west2369, comments; @windfuhr_perry2009]. TODO(verify): what the chapter says about Tajik.

Whether Tajik is a separate language also has a political side [@wp-tajik].

Tajikistan's 1989 language law made Tajik the state language and equated it with Persian. In 1999 the word fārsi was removed from that law [@wp-tajik-alphabet]. TODO(verify): the texts of both laws.

Afghanistan made the name Dari official in 1964. A study cited for this calls the renaming more political than linguistic (Green and Arbabzadah 2013) [@wp-dari].

The 1964 Constitution of Afghanistan names Pashto and Dari as official languages [@wp-dari]. TODO(verify): Article 35 and the constitution text. Wikipedia's Persian language article also gives 1958 in one section [@wp-persian-language].

Speakers in Tajikistan call their language [забони тоҷикӣ]{lang=tg-Cyrl} (zaboni tojikī). The phrase [форсии тоҷикӣ]{lang=tg-Cyrl} (forsii tojikī), "Tajik Persian", is also used [@wp-tajik].

## Story

Scholars divide the history of Persian into Old, Middle and New Persian [@wp-persian-language]. Where they cut New Persian into subperiods differs; see the two schemes below.

Glottolog files attested older stages beside the line, not on it. Old Persian (oldp1254) sits beside Middle-Modern Persian, and Pahlavi (pahl1241) beside Modern Southwestern Iranian [@glottolog53, languages.csv#sout3157, #midd1352].

### Old Persian: the Achaemenid inscriptions (about 520 BCE to about 330 BCE)

Old Persian is known from inscriptions. Behistun, in western Iran, is generally taken as the oldest [@wp-persian-language; @wp-behistun].

Inscriptions in the names of earlier kings exist, but they are generally agreed to be later. The script itself was created on Darius's orders [@wp-old-persian-cuneiform].

The end date of about 330 BCE follows Kent [@kent1953, p. 6]. TODO(verify): Kent was not opened.

Darius I had Behistun carved during his reign, 522 to 486 BCE [@wp-behistun, lead]. This atlas dates it to about 520 BCE.

The exact date of the Old Persian version is debated. It may have been added after the Elamite and Akkadian texts [@schmitt1991]. TODO(verify): Schmitt 1991 and Borger 1982 were not opened.

The inscription is in three languages: Old Persian, Elamite and Akkadian [@lrc-oldiranian-7, introduction].

It tells how Darius defeated ten rebel kings [@lrc-oldiranian-7, introduction].

**Sample, Behistun (DB IV 36)** [@lrc-oldiranian-7, lesson text, line 36]

- Transcription: [θātiy Dārayavauš xšāyaθiya]{lang=peo-Latn}
- Translation: "Darius the King declares."
- The same formula opens a passage in column I [@lrc-oldiranian-8, lesson text, lines 26 to 27].

**The word for king in its own script:** [𐎧𐏁𐎠𐎹𐎰𐎡𐎹]{lang=peo-Xpeo} (xšāyaθiya) [@wikt-xsayathiya]. TODO(verify): cuneiform for the full formula; the lesson gives transcription only.

### Middle Persian (about the 3rd century BCE to the 9th century CE; Zoroastrian books into the 10th and 11th)

The dates follow one summary of the stage [@wp-middle-persian].

It was the official, religious and literary language of the Sasanian empire, 224 to 651 CE [@wp-persian-language; @wp-middle-persian].

Most surviving Book Pahlavi texts were written down after the empire fell, in the 9th to 11th centuries. The manuscripts themselves are usually later copies [@wp-middle-persian].

Its grammar became more analytic. Nouns lost almost all their case endings, and prepositions took over their work [@wp-middle-persian].

Unstressed final syllables wore away. Old Persian [martiya-]{lang=peo-Latn} "man" became [mard]{lang=pal-Latn} [@wp-middle-persian].

**Sample, inscription of the priest Kartir (KZ 1)** [@wp-middle-persian, sec. Samples]

The priest's name appears as Kartir and as Kirdīr.

- Transliteration: [W ANE kltyl ZY mgwpt]{lang=pal-Latn}
- Transcription: [ud az Kirdīr ī mowbed]{lang=pal-Latn}
- Translation: "And I, Kirdir, the mowbed (chief of the magi)"

The Persian reading is a scholarly reconstruction [@wp-middle-persian, sec. Samples]. The Writing section explains why. TODO(verify): source the readings in MacKenzie 1971.

### Early New Persian

Persian texts in Arabic script first appear in the 9th century [@wp-persian-language].

Some of the oldest New Persian documents are in Hebrew script. Ludwig Paul calls this form Early Judaeo-Persian and dates it to roughly the 8th to 12th centuries [@paul2013_ejp].

One such letter was found in 1901 at Dandān-Uiliq, north of Khotan. It is dated "possibly eighth century" [@geniza-35176]. TODO(verify): whether Paul treats it as the earliest New Persian document.

Most accounts follow Gilbert Lazard on the origin of New Persian. It continues the spoken Persian of the Sasanian court, called darī [@lazard1975].

That speech spread to Khorasan. There it was first written in Arabic script and took in Parthian and other northeastern words [@lazard1975]. TODO(verify): Lazard 1975 was not opened.

### Classical Persian

Rudaki was born about 858 by one estimate and died in 940 or 941. He was a court poet of the Samanids in Bukhara [@wp-rudaki, lead].

In the conventional scheme he opens the Classical period; in Paul's he belongs to Early New Persian [@wp-persian-language; @paul2013_enp].

Iran calls him the founder of New Persian poetry. Tajikistan calls him the father of Tajik literature [@wp-rudaki].

**Sample, Rudaki, poem 121 in the Ganjoor edition, first half-line** [@ganjoor-rudaki121]

- Persian: بوی جوی مولیان آید همی
- Romanization (modern Iranian reading): bu-ye ju-ye muliyān āyad hami. TODO(verify).
- Translation: "The scent of the Muliyan stream keeps coming."

Ferdowsi began the Shahnameh, the "Book of Kings", in 977 and finished it in 1010 [@wp-shahnameh, sec. Composition].

**Sample, the Shahnameh's first line** [@ganjoor-shahnameh]

- Persian: به نام خداوندِ جان و خرد
- Romanization: be nām-e xodāvand-e jān o xerad. TODO(verify).
- Translation: "In the name of the Lord of soul and wisdom"

Saadi of Shiraz finished the Golestan, a book of prose and verse, in 1258 [@wp-gulistan, lead].

**Sample, Golestan, chapter 1, story 10** [@ganjoor-golestan]

- Persian: بنی آدم اعضایِ یکدیگرند
- Romanization: bani ādam aʿzā-ye yekdigarand. TODO(verify).
- Translation: "The children of Adam are limbs of one another."
- Many readers know a variant ending, اعضای یک پیکرند (aʿzā-ye yek peykarand), "limbs of one body". Ganjoor prints یکدیگرند, which the translation follows. TODO(verify): a source for the variant.

Persian became a language of state and literature from Anatolia to India. Historians call these societies "Persianate", a term coined by Marshall Hodgson in 1974 [@wp-persianate, lead].

Under the Mughal emperors, Persian was the official language [@wp-persian-language].

Under British rule, Persian lost its official role in the 1830s and 1840s. Sources differ on the year.

- Umesh Chandra dates the start to Act XXIX of 1837, which let the Governor-General replace Persian in court and revenue work. Madras and Bombay had partly switched by 1832 [@chandra-courtlanguage].
- Wikipedia's Persian language article describes a gradual replacement from 1843 [@wp-persian-language].
- TODO(verify): the text of Act XXIX of 1837.

## Sounds

*No recordings are attached to this page yet. Every future clip will carry a badge: Recording, Reconstruction or Synthetic.*

Iranian Persian has six vowels. One analysis writes them /æ e o/ and /ɒː iː uː/ [@wp-persian-phonology].

The same source notes that length no longer tells words apart in Iranian Persian [@wp-persian-phonology].

The sounds written ق and غ are usually pronounced alike in standard Iranian Persian. The usual result is [ɢ], often [ɣ] or [ʁ] between vowels [@wp-persian-phonology].

A word never starts with a bare vowel. A glottal stop comes first, as in آب [ʔɒːb̥] "water" [@wp-persian-phonology; @wikt-ab].

In nouns and adjectives, stress falls on the last syllable, plural endings included [@wp-persian-phonology; @kahnemuyipour2003].

Clitics such as the ezafe -e are never stressed [@wp-persian-phonology; @kahnemuyipour2003].

In verbs, the first prefix takes the stress, and the negative na- always does [@wp-persian-phonology]. TODO(verify): Kahnemuyipour 2003 was checked in its bibliography only.

A syllable starts with at most one consonant. Loanwords with initial clusters often gain an extra vowel [@wp-persian-phonology].

The other standards differ. Dari keeps the older eight-vowel system, with long and short vowels (Rastorgueva 1982, as summarized) [@wp-persian-phonology].

Standard Tajik has six vowels, and its dialects shift them in different ways [@wp-tajik].

### Word journeys out of Persian

**paradise.** An Old Iranian compound meaning "walled around" is attested in Avestan as [pairi.daēza-]{lang=ae-Latn} [@wikt-paridayjah; @wp-persian-gardens].

A western Iranian form of it was borrowed into Elamite, Akkadian ([pardēsu]{lang=akk-Latn}), Hebrew ([pardēs]{lang=he-Latn}) and Greek ([parádeisos]{lang=grc-Latn}) [@wikt-paridayjah; @wp-persian-gardens]. TODO(verify): a scholarly locator; Iranica "Paradise" returned 403.

Strictly, the source was an Old Iranian form, not necessarily Persian itself [@wikt-paradise, English etymology].

From Greek it went to Latin [paradīsus]{lang=la}, Old French [paradis]{lang=fro} and English paradise [@wikt-paradise, English etymology].

Modern Persian has پردیس (pardis, Classical pardēs). It means "garden", "paradise" and also "university campus" [@wikt-pardis].

**sugar.** Persian did not invent this word, but it passed it on. Sanskrit [śárkarā]{lang=sa-Latn} reached Middle Persian as [šakar]{lang=pal-Latn} [@wikt-sugar, English etymology].

From Persian it went to Arabic [sukkar]{lang=ar-Latn}, Old Italian [zucchero]{lang=it}, Old French [çucre]{lang=fro} and Middle English [sugre]{lang=enm} [@wikt-sugar, English etymology].

**kiosk.** Middle Persian [kōšk]{lang=pal-Latn} became Classical Persian کوشک (kōšk), "palace, portico" [@wikt-kiosk, English etymology].

Ottoman Turkish took it as [köşk]{lang=ota-Latn}. Italian made it [chiosco]{lang=it}, French [kiosque]{lang=fr}, and English kiosk [@wikt-kiosk, English etymology].

**pajamas.** Classical Persian had پایجامه (pāyjāma), an older form of پاجامه (pājāma) [@wikt-payjama; @wikt-pyjamas, English etymology].

Hindi and Urdu took it as [paijāmā]{lang=hi-Latn} and [pāijāma]{lang=ur-Latn}, and English took it from them [@wikt-pyjamas, English etymology]. TODO(verify): the breakdown pāy "leg" plus jāma "garment" needs an etymological dictionary.

**check (in chess).** Old Persian [xšāyaθiya]{lang=peo-Latn} "king" became Middle Persian [šāh]{lang=pal-Latn} [@wikt-mlka].

Persian شاه (šāh) went to Arabic [šāh]{lang=ar-Latn}, "king" and "check". Then came Medieval Latin [scaccus]{lang=la}, Old French [eschec]{lang=fro} and English check [@wikt-check, English etymology].

### Words that came in

Persian borrowed heavily from Arabic in the Middle Ages [@wp-persian-language].

One example: کتاب (ketāb, Dari [kitāb]{lang=prs-Latn}, Tajik [китоб]{lang=tg-Cyrl} kitob) "book", borrowed from Arabic [كتاب]{lang=ar} (kitāb) [@wikt-ketab].

Tajik took many words from Russian and Uzbek [@wp-tajik].

## Writing

### Now

Iranian Persian and Dari use the Perso-Arabic script [@wp-persian-language].

TODO(verify): the four letters Persian added to the Arabic alphabet (پ چ ژ گ); find a citable source.

Nastaliq is the classic calligraphic hand for Persian. It was developed for Persian poetry [@wp-nastaliq, sec. History].

Tajik uses a Cyrillic alphabet. A 1998 reform dropped four letters and left 35 [@wp-tajik-alphabet].

### Before

Old Persian was written in cuneiform, read left to right. It had 36 phonetic signs: three for vowels, the rest for a consonant plus a vowel [@lrc-oldiranian-7, introduction; @wp-old-persian-cuneiform].

It also had logograms for five words, among them "king", "country" and "god" [@lrc-oldiranian-7, introduction; @wp-old-persian-cuneiform].

Counting variant forms, those five words use eight signs [@devaan_lubotsky2011]. TODO(verify): page locator; the chapter was seen in a catalog only.

Middle Persian used scripts derived from Aramaic, chiefly the Pahlavi scripts [@wp-persian-language; @wp-middle-persian].

Pahlavi spelling used heterograms. A scribe wrote an Aramaic word and readers said the Persian one [@wp-middle-persian].

Example: [𐭬𐭫𐭪𐭠]{lang=pal-Phli} (MLKA), Aramaic for "king", was read [šāh]{lang=pal-Latn} [@wikt-mlka].

The Manichaean script, traditionally credited to Mani, spelled Middle Persian more regularly [@wp-middle-persian].

Tajik has had three scripts in a century. Perso-Arabic came first. A 1928 decree brought in a Latin alphabet, and Cyrillic followed in the late 1930s [@wp-tajik-alphabet].

Bukharian Jews traditionally wrote Bukhori in the Hebrew alphabet [@wp-tajik-alphabet, lead]. Glottolog lists it as Bukharic, a close relative of Tajik in its Tajikic group [@glottolog53, languages.csv#taji1250].

## Grammar DNA

Examples follow the cited source; the romanization is normalized to this page's scheme.

1. **Verb last.** The basic order is subject, object, verb [@wals-prs, feature 81A].
   کتاب آبی را دیدم (ketāb-e ābi rā didam), "I saw the blue book" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Word order].
2. **No grammatical gender** [@wals-prs, feature 30A].
   One pronoun, او (u), covers "he" and "she" [@wikt-u].
3. **The ezafe links a noun to what describes it.** The linker is a short -e [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Ezafe].
   کتابِ من (ketāb-e man), "my book", literally "book-of me" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Ezafe].
4. **Adjectives and relative clauses follow the noun** [@wals-prs, features 87A and 90A].
   ketāb-e ābi, "book blue", in example 1 [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Word order].
5. **An indefinite article but no definite one, in formal Persian** [@wals-prs, feature 37A].
   روی میز یک کتاب است (ru-ye miz yek ketāb ast), "There is a book on the table" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Articles]. TODO(verify): colloquial Persian's definite suffix -e.
6. **Prepositions, not postpositions** [@wals-prs, feature 85A].
   روی میز (ru-ye miz), "on the table" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Articles].
7. **No case endings on nouns** [@wals-prs, feature 51A]. In this example the word را (rā) follows the object.
   من کتاب را دوست دارم (man ketāb rā dust dāram), "I like the book" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Plural]. TODO(verify): the conditions for rā (definite or specific objects; Lazard 1992 not opened).
8. **Plural with -hā** (written here with a zero-width non-joiner).
   کتاب‌ها (ketāb-hā), "the books" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Plural].
9. **The verb carries the subject.** Endings mark the person, so pronouns are optional [@wals-prs, feature 101A; @wp-persian-grammar, sec. Pronouns].
   می‌خورم (mi-xor-am), "I eat" or "I am eating" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Tenses].
10. **Negation is a prefix** [@wals-prs, feature 112A].
    نخواهد خورد (na-xāhad xord), "she or he will not eat" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Tenses].
11. **Compound verbs.** Many verbs pair a noun with a light verb such as کردن (kardan), "to do" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Compound verbs].
    گریه کردن (gerye kardan), "to cry" [@wp-persian-grammar, sec. Compound verbs].
12. **Yes or no questions can open with a particle,** آیا (āyā) [@wals-prs, feature 116A; @wp-persian-grammar, sec. Word order].

## Connections

### Closest relatives

In Glottolog, Iranian Persian's closest relatives sit in the Farsic group: Judeo-Persian, and the Eastern Farsic languages [@glottolog53, languages.csv#fars1255].

Eastern Farsic holds Aimaq, Dari, Dehwari, Hazaragi and Pahlavani, plus the Tajikic group of Tajik and Bukharic [@glottolog53, languages.csv#east2745, #taji1250].

Caucasian Tat is the next branch out [@glottolog53, languages.csv#fars1254].

All of these belong to Southwestern Iranian [@glottolog53, languages.csv#west2369].

Farther out in the Iranian family are Northern Kurdish, in Northwestern Iranian, and the Pashto group [@glottolog53, languages.csv#nort2641, #nort2646].

### Contact languages

Persian borrowed heavily from Arabic, as the Words section shows [@wp-persian-language].

Persian in turn shaped Turkic languages, Armenian, Georgian, Greek and the Indo-Aryan languages [@wp-persian-language].

Persian loanwords are common in Hindi and Urdu, Punjabi, Kashmiri, Bengali and Sindhi [@wp-persian-language].

### The Persianate world

Seljuq, Timurid and Mughal courts used New Persian for government, scholarship and literature. Arabic stayed the language of religion [@wp-persianate, lead].

The Ottoman court also belonged to this world [@wp-persianate, lead]. Persian's role in Ottoman government was probably narrower. TODO(verify): a source for the Ottoman state's use of Persian.

Nastaliq traveled with it. Most Urdu writing uses it today [@wp-nastaliq].

### 1. The Behistun inscription and relief

A royal text carved on a cliff for Darius I, in Old Persian, Elamite and Akkadian [@lrc-oldiranian-7, introduction].

Henry Rawlinson began studying it in 1835. In 1847 he sent a full copy of the Old Persian text to Europe [@wp-behistun].

- Image candidate: [File:Behistun inscription reliefs.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Behistun_inscription_reliefs.jpg). Commons states: public domain. Note for rights-auditor: check the photographer's own rights.

### 2. The Shahnameh

Ferdowsi's Book of Kings runs to some 50,000 couplets [@wp-shahnameh, lead].

E. G. Browne held that Ferdowsi avoided Arabic words. Mohammed Moinfar counted about 700 Arabic-origin words, about 9% of its distinct words [@wp-shahnameh]. TODO(verify): Moinfar 1970 itself.

- Image candidate: ["The Feast of Sada", Folio 22v from the Shahnama (Book of Kings) of Shah Tahmasp, MET DP260189](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%22The_Feast_of_Sada%22,_Folio_22v_from_the_Shahnama_(Book_of_Kings)_of_Shah_Tahmasp_MET_DP260189.jpg). Attributed to Sultan Muhammad, about 1525. Commons states: CC0.

### 3. Nastaliq calligraphy

The name shortens nasx-e taʿliq, "hanging naskh" [@wp-nastaliq, lead].

Safavid writers credited Mir Ali Tabrizi, around 1400, with inventing it. Modern scholars describe a development over several centuries [@wp-nastaliq, sec. History].

- Image candidate: [File:Nastaliq calligraphy, Mir Emad, 11th century AH, Reza Abbasi Museum.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nastaliq_calligraphy,_Mir_Emad,_11th_century_AH,_Reza_Abbasi_Museum.jpg). Commons states: public domain.

### 4. The Divan of Hafez

Hafez was a 14th-century poet of Shiraz who wrote mainly ghazals. His Divan was probably compiled after his death [@wp-hafez, lead].

Many Iranians open the Divan at random and read the poem as a sign. This is called fāl-e hāfez [@wp-hafez].

The Divan's first line in the Ganjoor edition mixes two languages [@ganjoor-hafez1]. The first half-line is Arabic: [alā yā ayyuhā s-sāqī adir kaʾsan wa-nāwilhā]{lang=ar-Latn}. The second is Persian. TODO(verify): a citable statement of this.

- Second half-line: که عشق آسان نمود اوّل ولی افتاد مشکل‌ها (ke ešq āsān nemud avval vali oftād moškel-hā), "for love seemed easy at first, but then came the troubles". Romanization TODO(verify).

### 5. The Persian garden

Nine gardens in Iran were inscribed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, "The Persian Garden", in 2011 [@wp-persian-gardens].

The classic plan, the čahār bāġ ("four gardens"), splits the garden into four parts with water channels or paths [@wp-persian-gardens].

## Hear it then

*Audio for older stages, when added, will carry the Reconstruction badge with its evidence.*

**Lion and milk.** Scholars reconstruct Classical šēr "lion" and šīr "milk" with different vowels. The Arabic script spells both شیر.

Three kinds of evidence point to the split. Dari still has [ʃeːɾ] against [ʃiːɾ], and Tajik [шер]{lang=tg-Cyrl} against [шир]{lang=tg-Cyrl} [@wikt-shir].

Hindi borrowed the "lion" word as [शेर]{lang=hi} /ʃeːɾ/ [@wikt-hi-sher].

Rastorgueva (1982), as summarized in Persian phonology, describes Dari keeping the old eight-vowel system [@wp-persian-phonology].

Iranian Persian merged the two vowels. When the merger happened, the sources checked do not say. Confidence: high for the contrast, unknown for the date.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| شیر "lion" | šēr /ˈʃeːr/ | [ʃeːɾ] | [ʃiːɾ] | [шер]{lang=tg-Cyrl} [ʃeɾ] |
| شیر "milk" | šīr /ˈʃiːr/ | [ʃiːɾ] | [ʃiːɾ] | [шир]{lang=tg-Cyrl} [ʃiɾ] |

Sources: [@wikt-shir] for every cell except the Iranian Persian column. There this page writes a flap, [ɾ], as linguist-reviewer advised from Persian phonology [@wp-persian-phonology]. Wiktionary writes [ʃiːɹ].

**Paradise again.** Classical pardēs, a reconstruction, became Iranian pardis. Dari keeps [pardēs]{lang=prs-Latn} [@wikt-pardis].

**The letter و.** Classical و is reconstructed as [w]. Iranian Persian and Tajik moved to [v], not in every position. Dari keeps [w] [@wp-persian-phonology; @baker2016_dari]. TODO(verify): Baker 2016 was not opened.

## Open questions

**One language or three?** ISO groups Iranian Persian with Dari but not Tajik. Glottolog lists three languages. This atlas's lens follows scholars such as Perry who write of Tajik Persian [@wp-iso-macrolanguage; @glottolog53; @perry2005].

**How does New Persian relate to Middle Persian?** One debated point is whether New Persian is a later stage of written Middle Persian or a sister dialect [@lazard2003]. TODO(verify): Lazard 2003 was seen in a catalog only.

**Where does Early New Persian end?** The conventional scheme ends it in the 9th century; Paul (2013) runs it to the 12th [@wp-persian-language; @paul2013_enp]. TODO(verify): Paul's article.

**Why "Dari"?** Most scholars link the name to dar or darbār, "court". Gilbert Lazard is the main source cited [@wp-dari; @lazard1994_dari]. TODO(verify): Lazard's Encyclopaedia Iranica entry directly.

**How did Nastaliq form?** An older view says it combined naskh and taʿliq. Elaine Wright argued it grew from naskh alone (summarized by Sheila Blair, 2008) [@wp-nastaliq, sec. History].

**When was Hafez born?** Dates from 1315 to 1325 appear in the literature [@wp-hafez].

**How Arabic is the Shahnameh?** See Browne and Moinfar in the Culture shelf [@wp-shahnameh].

**When exactly was Behistun's Old Persian text carved?** See the Old Persian stage [@schmitt1991]. TODO(verify): Schmitt 1991 and Borger 1982.
